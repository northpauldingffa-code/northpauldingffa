The basics

What the Agriscience Fair is

The Agriscience Fair is a real research project. You ask a question about agriculture, run an experiment, and explain what you found to judges. Our goal this year is 2 or 3 strong North Paulding projects, and you could lead one of them.

  • Who can enter: FFA members enrolled in an ag class. Work alone or on a team of up to 3.
  • Grade levels: Grades 9–10 and grades 11–12 compete separately. A mixed team competes at the highest grade on the team.
  • Pathways: Animal Systems; Environmental Service/Natural Resource Systems; Food Products and Processing Systems; Plant Systems; Power, Structural and Technical Systems; and Social Science.
  • What you turn in: A written report on the National FFA report template.
  • How it is scored: The written report is 25% of your score. The interview with judges at state convention is 75%. Your display board is not scored.

That last point matters most. The judges want to hear you explain your own work for up to 15 minutes. If you did the work, you will be ready.

Step by step

How a research project works

A good project changes one thing, measures what happens, and repeats it enough times to trust the answer.

  1. Pick a problem. Choose something you care about and keep it narrow. “Plant growth” is too big. “Effect of soak time on radish microgreen yield” works.
  2. Write a hypothesis. Turn your question into a prediction you can test. Example: “Radish seeds soaked 8 hours will produce more grams per tray than unsoaked seeds.”
  3. Read what others found. Find at least 5 sources: Extension publications, university research, and ag journals. Save every link and title for your references.
  4. Design the experiment.
    • Independent variable: the one thing you change.
    • Dependent variable: what you measure, with units (grams, cm, days, %).
    • Control group: the normal or untreated version, to compare against.
    • Keep everything else the same: water, light, temperature, soil, seed lot.
    • Use enough samples: aim for 30 per group when possible, and mix up where pots or trays sit.
  5. Write a proposal (the form below) and get it approved before you buy anything.
  6. Collect data. Record every measurement in your logbook the day you take it. Write down anything odd you notice, too.
  7. Analyze. Find the average for each group and how spread out the numbers are. Make a graph.
  8. Draw a conclusion. Did the data support your hypothesis or not? Both are fine answers. Stay within what your data shows.
  9. Write the report on the National FFA template: abstract, introduction, literature review, materials and methods, results, conclusions, acknowledgements, and references.
  10. Practice the interview. Explain your project out loud to your advisor, an officer, and someone who knows nothing about it.
